    59→Chapter 1: Should I Get an Epidural? The Question That Looms Long Before Labor From the moment you announce your pregnancy, the opinions start pouring in: “You don’t need an epidural — your body was made for this.” “Girl, just get the drugs. Why suffer?” “Make a birth plan and stick to it.” “Don’t make a plan — it never goes that way!” The truth is: birth is unpredictable. But that doesn’t mean you’re powerless. This chapter is here to help you understand your options, hear a real story, and trust your instincts — no matter what path you take.
    60→
    61→What Is an Epidural? An epidural is a form of regional anesthesia that numbs pain from the waist down. It’s delivered through a catheter inserted into your lower back. Once it’s in place, pain medicine flows through the tube — usually with a button you can press to get more as needed. Common Effects: • Pain relief during contractions and pushing • Numbness or heaviness in the legs • Limited mobility (you may need a catheter or assistance) • Feeling pressure but not pain • A chance to rest and refocus during long or intense labor
    62→
    63→Pros and Cons Potential Benefits: • Relief from exhausting contractions • Helps you stay calm and conserve energy • May reduce tension in your body that could slow labor • Can allow for a more alert, peaceful birth if that’s what you desire Things to Consider: • May slow labor in some cases • Can cause a temporary drop in blood pressure • You may not feel when to push • Rare risks: spinal headache, long-term nerve irritation
    64→
    65→My Story: The Plan I Had… and How It Changed When I first found out I was pregnant, I was adamant about not getting an epidural. I asked every woman I knew who’d had a baby: “Did you get the epidural? Do you have back pain now?” I thought long and hard and told myself, “I’d rather feel pain for one day than deal with pain for the rest of my life.” I meant it. But labor has a way of humbling you. I was 18 hours in. My body was tired. I had been moaning through contractions and passing out in between them without even realizing it. I could barely keep my eyes open. At 7 centimeters, I cried to my husband: “I can’t do it, baby. I need the epidural.” Suddenly the nurses were in motion — like it was a national emergency. They asked me one last time if I was sure, and when I said yes, it was all hustle: “Call anesthesia,” “Get the meds,” “Prep her now.” I didn’t know the epidural meant a tube would stay in my back. I didn’t know they’d give me a button to push for extra doses. I pressed that button twice — and then they cut me off. “Your blood pressure is too high, they said. We can’t give you more right now.” I felt defeated. The medicine started to wear off, and I still had a ways to go. Looking back now? I still say this: I wouldn’t wish contractions on my worst enemy.
    66→
    67→What I Learned Making the choice to get an epidural didn’t make me weak. It didn’t erase my strength or take away the sacredness of my birth. It was what I needed in the moment — and I gave myself permission to change my mind. That is strength, too. Reflection Prompts • What have I heard or assumed about epidurals? • Am I afraid of pain — or afraid of feeling like I’ve failed? • How can I give myself grace to make the right choice when the time comes? “I will meet my labor with strength, softness, and self-trust. No matter how I birth, I will do it with courage.”

    69→Chapter 2: What If My Baby Comes Early or Late? Due Dates Are a Guess, Not a Guarantee You circle the date on the calendar. You imagine what you’ll wear. What song will play. What the weather will feel like when your baby arrives. But the truth? Only 4–5% of babies are born on their due date. Some come early. Some come late. Some come like a whisper. Some like a lightning storm. This chapter is your permission slip to release the calendar and embrace the unknown — not with fear, but with faith in your body’s wisdom and your baby’s timing.
    70→
    71→What Happens If Baby Comes Early? A birth before 37 weeks is considered preterm. Sometimes this happens due to: • Preeclampsia or high blood pressure • Premature rupture of membranes • Infections or complications • No clear reason at all Early doesn’t always mean an emergency. Some babies just need a little more support — like NICU time to breathe, grow, or feed. Others are born healthy and strong, just smaller and sooner than expected. What you should know: Your birth is still valid. Your baby is still strong. You didn’t fail.
    72→
    73→What Happens If Baby Comes Late? A pregnancy that goes past 41 weeks is called “post-term.” This might mean: • More monitoring (non-stress tests, ultrasounds) • A discussion about induction • Watching for signs like decreased movement or low amniotic fluid Sometimes waiting is safe. Sometimes induction is wise. But it’s okay to feel tired, anxious, done with being pregnant. You haven’t done anything wrong. Some babies just need extra time to bake.
    74→
    75→My Story: A Bond with Every Bump During pregnancy, I noticed something beautiful — a quiet sisterhood. It didn’t matter where I was — at work, in the grocery store, in a parking lot — if I saw another pregnant woman, we’d smile at each other like we already knew each other’s story. There was some unwritten rule that said, “We’re in this together.” And every time we talked, most of the women — especially those in the third trimester — would say, “I’m just so ready. I’m tired. This baby needs to come out!” Eventually, I felt the same. I was uncomfortable. My hips were stretching, my belly got in the way of everything, and I hadn’t slept on my stomach in months. My legs were restless at night, and I’d wake up between 3 and 4 a.m. starving every single morning. I was physically READY. And emotionally UNDONE. At least, that’s what I thought. 35 Weeks: The Day Everything Changed It was a normal OB appointment. My nurses were laughing and joking with me, just like usual. They checked my vitals, ran a few tests, and then left the room. When they returned, everything was different. One nurse looked at me and said gently, “We’re going to have to deliver your baby today.” I laughed. “Quit playing with me.” But they weren’t laughing. “We’re not joking. You’ve developed preeclampsia. You’re going to meet your little guy either tonight or tomorrow.” I was in shock. My blood pressure had been fine just three weeks earlier. How could this be real? I was alone. My husband didn’t come to that appointment because he wasn’t feeling well. The nurses stepped out to grab a wheelchair for the transfer to labor and delivery, and as soon as the door shut behind them… I cried. I called my husband. I could barely get the words out: “I have to have the baby… today.” He stumbled over his words and said, “I’m on my way.” When I was wheeled to labor and delivery, the nurses there were so kind. They laid out the plan, answered my questions, and gave me back a sense of calm in the middle of total surprise.
    76→
    77→Trusting the Mystery I didn’t feel ready. But my body and my baby were following a plan I couldn’t see. And that plan — though scary and sudden — brought me to the love of my life. Whether your baby comes “early,” “late,” or “right on time” — it will be your story. And it will be beautiful. Reflection Prompts • How do I feel about not knowing when my baby will come? • What helps me stay grounded in the unknown? • If things don’t go as planned, what’s one way I can still feel empowered? “My baby will arrive at the perfect time. I release control and trust the unfolding.”

    79→Chapter 3: What Is Labor Really Like? You Can Prepare… But You Can’t Predict You can read the books, take the classes, rehearse your breathing, and visualize your perfect moment — but labor rarely goes the way we expect. It’s more than pain. It’s emotional, primal, and transformative. Every birth is different — and every single one is sacred. I Was in It There’s a moment in labor when the world stops making sense. Time bends. Voices blur. The walls breathe. And the only thing real is the feeling that your body is doing something ancient and unstoppable. I remember contractions coming like thunder. I’d moan low, deep in my chest, as if that was the only sound I had left in me. Between them, I blacked out — literally passed out — just to come back and brace myself for the next wave. There was no thinking. No talking. No “birth playlist.” Just instinct. Just breathe. Just here. What I Thought It Would Be Like I had a vision. I thought I’d labor naturally. No epidural. No medicine. I’d ride the pain like a wave and breathe my way through it. I thought I was prepared. I thought I was strong enough to feel every inch of it. And I was strong. But strength looks different in the thick of it. What Actually Happened At 7 centimeters, I cried to my husband, “I can’t do it. I need the epidural.” The look in his eyes told me he was being strong, but he was scared and they were almost pleading with me to make it through no matter what it took. I didn’t know it involved a tube in my back. Or a button I’d press for relief. I pressed it twice. Then they cut me off. “Your blood pressure’s too high,” they said. “We can’t give you more.” It was like being dropped back into the ocean after coming up for air. I felt defeated. But I also felt ready. The Final Stretch My son was in my uterus head down, but facing the ceiling. My doctor told me to roll over and lay on the side of my belly so that he would flip over to face the floor. After a while I felt him turn his whole body over inside of me as if he knew that’s what it would take to come home to Mommy. I felt him travel through my body and I knew… he was coming. My family called in the doctor… she said “yep, he’s coming alright.” She told me to wait for her cue. Follow her instructions. I pushed three times. And there he was. Aftermath I was stitched. Exhausted. Shaking. Someone laid him on my chest. He cried. Then I spoke. And just like that — he stopped. He looked at me. Lifted his tiny head. His eyes stared straight into mine. And I knew. He knew me. I knew him. We were no longer strangers. I had just met my favorite human in the world.
    80→
    81→No Lesson. Just Truth. I can’t wrap this chapter in a bow. I can’t tell you what your labor will be like. I won’t list centimeters or stages. Because labor isn’t a chart — it’s a transformation. It will break you. It will find you. And it will give you back to yourself — brand new, and still you.

    87→The Day I Became a Mother   Chapter 4: My Birth Story — Preeclampsia, Pain, and Tatum The Surprise That Changed Everything I was 35 weeks pregnant when I walked into my OB appointment expecting the usual: blood pressure, heartbeat check, maybe a reminder to start thinking about hospital bags. What I got instead was something I never imagined — an immediate diagnosis of preeclampsia. My blood pressure was dangerously high. And I wasn’t going home. They told me I’d be induced that day. Everything after that felt like being swept into a current I couldn’t control. First, they had to get my blood pressure under control. I lay there, holding back tears and trying to process that I was about to give birth to my baby five weeks early. He wasn’t ready. I wasn’t ready. But ready or not, the wave had begun. Eighteen Hours of Labor I was in labor for 18 hours. The contractions came on slow at first, like gentle warnings, but by the time active labor hit, I lost track of time. Hours melted into each other. I felt like I was floating in and out of myself. Toward the end, I actually passed out between contractions and didn’t even realize it. The nurses would call my name, and I’d open my eyes like I’d just come up for air. It was like time stopped — but not in a peaceful, magical way. In a survival way. The pain pulled me inward. Every moan was a signal: I’m still here. I’m trying. I had told everyone — my doctor, my mom, my husband — that I didn’t want an epidural. I had said it for months. I meant it… until I didn’t. Somewhere around 7 centimeters, I broke. I cried out to my husband: “I can’t take it anymore!” He looked at me with so much love and didn’t question me for a second. Soon after, I had the epidural, and for the first time, I could breathe again. The Silence, the Energy, the Edge At one point, I didn’t want anyone talking around me anymore. Every voice felt too loud, every question too sharp. I didn’t want to be rude, but I was on the edge. I even told my mom to tell the doctor to tie my tubes because at that moment I knew I never wanted to feel this again. No one took me seriously. But at the time, I meant it. I didn’t scream much during labor. Each contraction stole too much energy from me. I just moaned — low, primal sounds that felt like the only thing my body had the strength to do. But when it came time to push… something in me snapped awake. The Moment of Becoming The room changed. The voices grew more urgent. And suddenly, it was time. I pushed once — the doctor encouraged me. I pushed again — and then I yelled: “GET HIM OUT!!” That third push was mine. I locked in. I remembered what women told me before — “Push like you’re pooping.” So I did. And in that final moment of complete surrender and power, Tatum was born. Everyone Was Talking — Except Me Everyone around me started buzzing. The doctor gave instructions. The nurses cleaned and weighed him. My family spoke to me, but it was all a blur. I just heard voices. And I just lay there. Still. Exhausted. Waiting. I had brought life into the world, and now I was hollowed out and waiting to be filled again. The doctor gave me a few stitches. I winced and said quietly, “Please don’t touch me anymore.” I meant it. Everything hurt. My body was no longer mine. The Moment I Met My Son And then... They laid him on my chest. Crying. And I spoke to him. He stopped crying immediately. He lifted his tiny head, eyes the color of coal but clear like glass, and he looked right at me. That moment didn’t feel like a fairytale. It felt like truth. It felt like: He knows I’m his mommy. And I knew right then, with every aching cell in my body — He is my favorite human in the whole wide world. “He is my favorite human in the whole wide world.”

    93→Chapter 5: Feeding — Breast, Bottle, Formula Feeding Isn’t Just Physical — It’s Emotional You will make dozens of choices as a new mom. Few feel as tender — or as judged — as how you choose to feed your baby. People will tell you breast is best. Others will tell you fed is best. But here’s what’s actually best: A calm, healthy, supported mama and a full-bellied baby. You are allowed to change your mind. You are allowed to try everything. You are allowed to do what works — for you, for your baby, for your body, and for your soul.
    94→
    95→Breastfeeding — A Bond and a Battle Breastfeeding is often described as natural. But natural doesn’t mean easy. What to expect: • Your milk may take 2–5 days to come in. • Latching takes time (for you and baby). • Your nipples may feel raw or sore at first. • You might feel overwhelmed or even touched out. What helps: • Skin-to-skin right after birth • Breast compressions during feeds • Nipple cream (lanolin or coconut oil) • Support from a lactation consultant What no one says enough: • If you cry during feeds, you’re not alone. • If you stop breastfeeding, you’re still a good mother. • If it becomes joyful, you’ll remember that forever. “I didn’t know it would hurt. I didn’t know it would feel so powerful. I didn’t know it would heal me.” — Mama Amina
    96→
    97→Bottle Feeding — Flexibility, Freedom, and Shared Snuggles Some mamas bottle-feed pumped breast milk. Others use formula. Many do both. Bottle feeding allows: • Partners, grandparents, or siblings to help • You to track exactly how much baby is eating • A more flexible routine Things to know: • Use paced bottle feeding to mimic breastfeeding flow • Watch for signs of overfeeding (spitting up, fussiness, milk pooling) • Always hold your baby upright and make it bonding time, not just a task You might feel guilt. You might feel relief. You might feel nothing at all. That’s okay.
    98→
    99→Formula Feeding — Nourishment With No Apology Formula is safe, regulated, and nourishing. Some mamas start on formula. Some transition. Some come to it after painful tries at breastfeeding. Choosing formula: • Start with standard cow’s milk-based unless baby has sensitivities • Powder is most cost-effective, but ready-to-feed is most convenient • Mix carefully (always follow water-then-formula rule) Common Challenges (and Gentle Encouragement) Engorgement? Apply cold packs. Nurse often. Use cabbage leaves. (Yes, really.) Low supply? Try oatmeal, lactation cookies, hydration, and nursing on demand. Guilt? Breathe. Then say out loud: “I am feeding my baby with love. I am not failing.” Judgment? Smile. Nod. Move on. You owe no one an explanation.
   100→
   101→My Experience: From Bottles to Nursing At first, I alternated between nursing and bottle-feeding my baby. It seemed to make sense. But after a few weeks, I noticed the spit-up, the discomfort, the endless night wakeups. He had reflux — and he was overfeeding. I didn’t know bottle-feeding made that easier to happen. I didn’t know he was drinking too much, too fast. Eventually, I switched to exclusive nursing. It wasn’t easy. It wasn’t perfect. But it helped. I watched my baby settle more quickly after feeding. I learned his cues. I learned myself. More than anything, I learned this: It’s okay to pivot. It’s okay to not know. It’s okay to change your plan for your baby — and for yourself. Reflection Prompts • What kind of feeding experience do I hope for? • How do I feel when I think about breastfeeding? Bottle-feeding? • What kind of support would help me most? “I feed my baby with love. I feed my baby with patience. I feed my baby with my whole heart.”

   103→Chapter 6: How to Latch — And When It’s Hard The Art of the Latch Latching is the first step to a successful breastfeeding experience — and one of the most frustrating parts for many new moms. You’d think something so instinctual would come naturally. But often, it doesn’t. And mama, that doesn’t mean you’re doing it wrong. Your baby is learning. You’re learning. You’re both new at this. Latching isn’t about perfection. It’s about patience. What a Good Latch Looks (and Feels) Like A good latch means: • Baby’s mouth is wide open • Lips are flanged out (like a fish) • More of the areola is in the baby’s mouth — not just the nipple • You feel pulling and tugging, not sharp pain • Baby’s chin touches your breast, and the nose is free You may hear swallowing sounds — that’s a good sign. You should NOT feel: • Pinching • Sharp stinging • Bleeding or cracked nipples (especially after the first week) A latch that hurts is a latch that can be adjusted. Positioning Tips for a Better Latch Start with the basics: • Hold baby tummy-to-tummy • Bring baby to breast — not breast to baby • Use your hand to shape your breast into a “sandwich” to help them latch Common positions: • Cradle hold – Baby rests in your arm, tummy facing you • Football hold – Baby is tucked beside you, great for c-section recovery • Side-lying – Rest together on your sides, helpful during night feeds • Laid-back / Biological nursing – You recline and baby leads the way Try different ones. What works today may not work tomorrow. And that’s okay. When Latching is Really, Really Hard Here are some truths no one told me: • Sometimes your baby will scream and resist. • Sometimes you’ll cry while trying. • Sometimes it will feel like rejection. • Sometimes your nipples will crack or bleed. • Sometimes, it’s too much. And still — you are doing incredible.
   104→
   105→When to Ask for Help Call a lactation consultant if: • Pain persists beyond the first week • Baby is not gaining weight • Your nipples are severely damaged • Feedings last over an hour and baby still seems hungry • You’re feeling discouraged and unsure You are never weak for needing support. You are a smart, strong mama for reaching out. National Breastfeeding Helpline: 1-800-994-9662 My Story: Learning the Hard Way (And Laughing Through It) I thought I was prepared. I took the breastfeeding classes. I watched the videos. I practiced with a doll. But none of it could teach me what actual experience taught me. It took me six weeks — yes, six weeks — to finally get a latch that didn’t make me wince. And that’s after I’d already tried pumping. But the most magical thing about breastfeeding? My body tells me when my baby is ready to eat. It’s a sensation I can’t quite describe — like a wave, a pull, almost telekinetic. My breast will feel something, and I’ll turn to my husband and say: “It’s time for Tatum to eat.” And like clockwork — Tatum wakes up. Hungry. Ready. It’s like we’re still connected, even though we’re no longer one body. Breastfeeding wasn’t just something I did — it became a language only we spoke. Reflection Prompts • What’s been the hardest part of feeding for me so far? • How do I feel when latching goes well? • What kind of support do I need more of? “My baby and I are learning together. We will get there, one gentle moment at a time.”

   107→Chapter 7: Reflux, Overfeeding, and Our Story When Feeding Doesn’t Feel Right You feed your baby. You burp him. You swaddle him. And then… he’s still uncomfortable. Maybe it’s not spit-up this time. Maybe it’s the crying, the grunting, the stiff little legs. Something isn’t right — but it’s hard to explain. That’s the frustration of reflux. And sometimes, it’s not even the visible kind. And suddenly, something as nurturing as feeding becomes… stressful. What Is Infant Reflux? Reflux is when milk or acid flows back up from the stomach into the esophagus. It happens to almost all babies at some point and it’s super common in babies under 6 months — their digestive systems are still developing. But silent reflux is sneakier. Signs of reflux include: • Frequent spit-ups (more than 1–2 per feeding) • Arching of the back or fussiness during/after feeds • Coughing or gagging • Hiccups after feeding • Crying while laying flat • Poor weight gain (in some cases) Signs of Silent Reflux: • Grunting or squirming during or after feeds • Arched back, clenched fists • Wet burps or gurgling sounds • Discomfort when lying flat • Crying without visible spit-up It’s reflux — just without the mess. Bottle-fed babies, especially, may not know when to stop. Their sucking reflex is so strong that if a bottle is in their mouth, they’ll keep drinking — even if their tummy’s already full. Our Story: We Tried Everything At first, I did what I was told. My son was born small — a preemie — and we were told to fortify my breastmilk with formula so he could gain weight faster. I did it. And I watched him become uncomfortable, fussy, and unsettled. Then came the suggestion: a feeding schedule. Every 3 hours. Strict. But here’s what they didn’t see: After just one hour, he would cry. And I mean cry. Not fussy. Not tired. Crying that pierced the room and shattered my heart. I tried to follow the schedule. But every cell in my body said: “This isn’t right. Feed your baby.” Fortified Milk and Our Breaking Point I had been adding formula to my breastmilk — just like the NICU team said. But after everything we were seeing and hours of research and reading reviews, we threw the formula away. I had to trust that my milk was enough. I had to believe in my baby’s cues, not the schedule. I had to unlearn the idea that more ounces = better feeding. Because babies don’t know how to regulate their intake from a bottle. If it’s there, they’ll drink it. It wasn’t his fault. It wasn’t mine either. We just needed a different way. We Changed Everything So we did. We switched to on-demand feeding. We stopped fortifying. We tuned in instead of timing out.    111→Chapter 9: When Baby Gets Sick or Colic Hits The First Cough Feels Like a Crisis Your baby cries all the time — but this cry is different. It’s sharper. More desperate. Something isn’t right.    115→Chapter 11: Sleep Tips for Exhausted Moms Sleep: The Golden Unicorn Sleep, once taken for granted, becomes the most sacred, elusive, impossible thing once you become a mother. You hear: “Sleep when the baby sleeps.” And you think: Okay but when do I shower? Eat? Cry? Exhaustion isn’t just physical. It’s emotional. It’s mental. It’s a fog that sits behind your eyes, follows you through every diaper change, and turns every small task into a mountain. This chapter isn’t about making sleep perfect. It’s about making it possible — and giving yourself permission to rest. Baby Sleep Basics Newborns sleep a lot — 14 to 17 hours a day. But it’s in short bursts, mostly 2–3 hours at a time. Why? • Their stomachs are tiny (they wake to eat) • They don’t know day from night yet • Their nervous systems are still developing Safe Sleep 101 (Always!): • Place baby on their back to sleep • Use a firm, flat surface with no pillows, bumpers, or blankets • Room-share, but do not bed-share • Use a swaddle or sleep sack for warmth — not loose blankets How to Help Your Baby Sleep (Gently) • Create a wind-down routine: bath, lullaby, dim lights, soft voice • Try white noise or soft ocean sounds • Use blackout curtains to signal night • Swaddle snugly (until baby rolls) • Feed baby right before bed • Keep nighttime feeds low-stimulation (no bright lights, minimal talking) You don’t need a perfect system. Just a few cues that say, “It’s time to rest now.” How to Help You Sleep Here’s the honest truth: You won’t sleep “like before” — not for a while. But you can still rest. Still recharge. Still protect your sanity. Micro-Rest Ideas: • Close your eyes for 10 minutes with baby on your chest (someone watching you both) • Nap during one daytime feed (partner or friend gives a bottle) • Lay down instead of doing chores when baby naps • Go to bed when the baby does — even if it’s 8 p.m. • Ask someone to hold baby while you nap unapologetically My Story: Relearning What Rest Means I used to think rest meant 8 hours of sleep. Sometimes it’s a midday nap. Sometimes it’s crashing at 8 p.m. Sometimes it’s just closing my eyes and breathing for ten minutes. It’s not perfect. But as a stay-at-home mom, it works. I rest enough to function. To show up. To love my baby with a little more energy in my tank. And that’s what matters. Reflection Prompts • What belief do I have about rest that no longer serves me? • How can I give myself more permission to pause? • Who can I ask for support — even just for one nap? “I rest because I deserve to heal.
